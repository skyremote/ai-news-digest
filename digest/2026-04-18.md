## Executive Summary

Snap's decision to cut 16% of its workforce while disclosing that AI now writes 65% of its new code is the clearest corporate signal yet that productivity gains are translating directly into headcount reductions, not just efficiency margins. DeepSeek R2's release—a 32-billion-parameter open-weight reasoning model matching frontier-level math benchmarks at 70% lower API cost—puts sustained pressure on the Western lab pricing floor. Against that backdrop, Stanford's 2026 AI Index documents a disquieting inversion: capability benchmarks are breaking records while model transparency scores have fallen sharply, meaning we know less about how these systems work even as they grow more consequential.

The annual report finds frontier models now exceed 50% on Humanity's Last Exam and near 100% on SWE-bench Verified—one year of gains that would have seemed implausible in 2024—while the Foundation Model Transparency Index average dropped from 58 to 40, and documented AI incidents rose from 233 to 362.

## Signal, Not Noise

**The Stanford transparency inversion.**

The Foundation Model Transparency Index score falling from 58 to 40 in a single year is the finding that deserves more attention than it is getting. Labs are disclosing less—about training data, evaluation methodology, and safety testing—precisely as models become capable enough to matter in high-stakes domains. The practical consequence is that independent safety researchers are working with less ground truth, regulators are assessing systems they cannot audit, and benchmark results are increasingly difficult to contextualise. The capability headlines are loud; the transparency trend is quiet and more durable.
*—[Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/inside-the-ai-index-12-takeaways-from-the-2026-report)*
